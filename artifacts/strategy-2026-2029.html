Risk taxonomy

Separately from the org structure above, risk itself is classified in a taxonomy the department also operates within:

Level 1 — Operational Risk, the enterprise root.
Level 2 — 16 domains; Technology is one, alongside Business Resiliency, Model Risk, Fraud, Tax, Legal, and others still to confirm.
Within Technology — 12 domains (IT Operations, Cybersecurity, Architecture & Innovation, IT Risk Management, SDLC, Strategy & Planning, six more to confirm), with 45 Level 3 sub-categories in aggregate.

Third-Party Risk, Cloud Computing, and Artificial Intelligence sit outside this hierarchy as transversal categories — asset/risk types that cut across every domain.

Centralization posture

The recurring answer across every fragmentation instance is the same: not full centralization, but a hybrid model — standardize the required activity and methodology centrally, keep delivery federated where genuinely necessary. Where duplication has no such justification (US Issues Management, a legacy of the regulatory-remediation era), the direction is straightforward consolidation.

Proof pointControl Testing (F3.1) is already run as one genuinely centralized, consistent team for all IT-related control testing bank-wide — a working example of the target model.

Reference · 15

Three themes that recur everywhere

These surfaced independently across nearly every region and pillar. They belong at the macro level of the strategy — the pillar and regional detail later in this document are largely instances of one of the three.

A — Federated fragmentation & non-standardization

Internal to IT Risk Management
ProcessPeople

A large number of federated teams — across regions and the center — overlap in remit, occasionally reach conflicting risk conclusions, and operate without standardized activities or methodology. Root cause is historical accretion, not deliberate design. This undermines the department's core deliverable: a single, consistent operational risk profile.

EvidenceSecond Line of Defense has raised concerns on the quality of self-identified issues — completeness, accuracy, and comprehensiveness.

Known instances

  • US local Issues Management team duplicating the central Issues Management function (F6.3 vs. F5)
  • Operational Risk's internal reporting duplicating the central Reporting & Analytics team (F2.1 vs. F3.2)
  • Third Party Risk producing its own reporting, only partially integrated with the center
  • Risk-control lineage inconsistency: teams locally recreate a centralized change-management control rather than referencing it

Target direction is explicitly not full centralization — a hybrid, semi-federated model: standardize methodology and required activities centrally, keep execution federated.

B — Legal-entity-aligned reporting

Regulatory driver
PeopleProcessTechnology

The department's default operating model is platform-aligned. US regulatory requirements mandate legal-entity-level governance and reporting instead — a structural constraint, not a choice. The mismatch produces increased reporting variants, longer cycle times, and operational risk from inconsistent sub-entity-to-parent rollups.

Live today in the US (F6). Emerging in Europe (F7) — business expansion brings direct ECB supervisory engagement in roughly 12–18 months, extending legal-entity reporting from the UK alone to Germany, France, and potentially others.

Strategic intent: solve once, as a reusable pattern, rather than re-solving per region as the requirement spreads.

C — Data foundation & reporting narrative capability

Remediation in flight
TechnologyPeople

The same two weaknesses — inconsistent/poorly structured underlying data, and a reporting capability too junior or too narrow to turn data into a clear narrative — recur independently in Operational Risk, Assessments/RCSA, Reporting & Analytics, and Third Party Risk. Worth treating as one department-wide capability gap rather than four unrelated ones.

Open itemWhether this expansion is funded with new capacity, given the Reporting arm is already understaffed.

Control testing digitization

Live across seven platforms and thousands of controls, with an AI model handling transcription and parsing of control walkthroughs. Expanding toward automated quality analysis — a copilot working alongside the tester.

Next phase — automated risk profile & Control Strength

Generate a risk profile at any level of the hierarchy — legal entity, business unit, platform, function — automatically, scored 1–6 at every level.

Control Strength

Calculated per control, from weighted factors: design (Test of Design), effectiveness (Test of Effectiveness), whether the corresponding KRI/KCI is within appetite, open issues against it (negative factor), and whether the control is automated (bonus weighting).
